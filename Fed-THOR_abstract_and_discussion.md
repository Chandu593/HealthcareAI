## Abstract

**Background.** Hospitals that want a shared ICU risk model usually cannot pool patient records. **Methods.** We study federated learning on the eICU Collaborative Research Database, treating each hospital as a client (204 to 206 of 207 hospitals contribute to a given model), for in-hospital mortality (primary) and a proxy-labelled sepsis task (secondary). Inputs are 696 features (for mortality) from the first 24 hours of the stay, with vocabularies and scaling fitted on training data only, on one fixed hospital-wise split. The primary protocol is a strict 24-hour landmark cohort (131,695 stays). We compare federated logistic regression, a federated MLP (FedAvg with a FedProx term) and a federated histogram gradient-boosted tree model in which clients send gradient and hessian histograms, against local-only, centrally trained and pooled-LightGBM references. The headline model is the federated arm with the highest validation AUROC. **Results.** On the strict cohort the federated tree model reaches a test AUROC of 0.875 (95% CI 0.866 to 0.882), against 0.862 for the federated MLP, 0.869 for a centrally trained MLP, 0.880 for pooled LightGBM and 0.742 for local-only MLPs. On identical patients it is 0.013 above the federated MLP and 0.005 below pooled LightGBM; the same ordering holds with all stays (0.907) and on both sepsis windows (0.860 at 24 hours, 0.857 at 6 hours). The federated tree model matches the same tree code trained on pooled data (AUROC differences of -0.0002 to +0.0006 in five of six settings, all intervals including zero; -0.006 averaged over three seeds in a 3-hospital federation, where we neither claim nor rule out a cost), and its 0.002 to 0.005 gap to pooled LightGBM is also found with pooled training. Federation gains 0.07 to 0.12 over isolation, depending on the measure. In federations of the size used by a recent eICU study (3 and 10 hospitals) the tree model reaches 0.8635 and 0.8662; its advantage over federated logistic regression is resolved at 10 hospitals (+0.015) but not at 3. Scoring the same predictions against ICU-discharge death, the label used by the closest prior study, raises AUROC by 0.013 to 0.034 (paired intervals exclude zero). At a validation-chosen threshold the federated tree model has accuracy 0.889 and F1 0.472 on the strict cohort, and F1 0.439 and 0.470 in the 3- and 10-hospital federations, below the 0.59 to 0.65 reported by the prior study, whose prevalence, label and test-tuned threshold differ. The sepsis label is a proxy (29% precision and 50% recall against documented sepsis), and the proxy-label gain does not carry over to documented sepsis. **Conclusions.** Federated gradient boosting is a strong, simple baseline for multi-hospital ICU prediction, at roughly 40 to 50 times the communication of a federated MLP. We make no privacy claim (no differential privacy or secure aggregation) and no claim of superiority over published figures that use a different label, cohort and test set.

## Discussion

**What the data resolve.** Three results are separated from noise by paired intervals on identical test patients. The federated tree model beats the federated MLP in all four settings (+0.013 strict, +0.015 all stays, +0.018 sepsis at 24 and at 6 hours) [Table 10, Section 3]. Federation costs little against pooling the same model family: the MLP loses about 0.007 to 0.008 against its central version, and the tree model nothing resolvable against the same code trained on pooled data in five of six settings (differences between -0.0002 and +0.0006, intervals including zero) [Table 13]. Its 0.002 to 0.005 gap to pooled LightGBM is also present with pooled training, so it belongs to our implementation and search, not to federation [Section 2, items 3 and 5]. The exception is the 3-hospital federation (-0.010 at seed 42, -0.006 averaged over three seeds), a gap about the size of the seed spread, where we neither claim nor rule out a cost. And federation helps much more than isolation: local-only MLPs reach 0.742 pooled AUROC, a gain of +0.07 to +0.12 for federation depending on whether scores are compared pooled, within hospital, or after per-hospital intercept calibration [item 4]. The federated MLP also exceeds APACHE IVa predicted mortality by 0.035 on the 15,151 test patients that have it [item 7].

**Relation to earlier federated eICU studies.** The closest study, FLICS (2026), also uses eICU only, so we use it as the base paper and exclude studies on other or mixed databases. The comparison is indirect and we draw no conclusion of superiority from it. FLICS predicts death at ICU discharge from whole-stay summaries, on federations of 3 or 10 hospitals, and scores 1,000 patients, so its reference interval is about ±0.035; its hospital tables also differ from the real eICU hospitals (its largest has 8,000 stays against 7,058 here), and its reported mortality of 12.5 to 17% exceeds the 9.0% in-hospital rate [Section 5]. We predict in-hospital death from first-24-hour features. To remove the difference in federation size, we re-trained every model on only the 3 and the 10 largest hospitals [Table 11a]. The federated tree model is within the uncertainty of FLICS's 3-hospital figure and numerically higher than its 10-hospital figures (by 0.026 to 0.036), a difference smaller than FLICS's own interval. The outcome label also matters. Scored against ICU-discharge death, the same predictions reach a higher AUROC in every setting we checked, by 0.013 to 0.034, with paired intervals that exclude zero [Table 12]. For the 3-hospital logistic regression the point estimate of this change (+0.034, interval +0.007 to +0.062) is more than twice the gap to FLICS's reported 0.87 (0.015), although the lower end of the interval is below that gap. The models were trained on in-hospital death, one seed was scored, and FLICS's cohort and features differ, so this shows that the label can account for differences of the size seen between the studies, not that it does.

**Accuracy and F1.** In FLICS's format [Table 14] the federated tree model is the best federated model on accuracy, AUROC and F1 in all six settings, and pooled LightGBM is the best central model on AUROC in all six; accuracy and F1 rankings are descriptive, because their differences are mostly inside the seed spread. Our F1 is lower than FLICS's reported 0.59 to 0.65 (0.439 at 3 hospitals and 0.470 at 10, against 0.60 to 0.61 and 0.59 to 0.61 for its federated models), and we draw no conclusion from that. Both metrics depend on prevalence (9.1 to 9.7% here, 12.5 to 17.0% there), on the label, and on the threshold: FLICS tunes it on its test set, we fix it on validation, which cannot give a higher F1. FLICS's centralized XGBoost (AUC 0.90 and 0.88) is nominally above our pooled LightGBM and pooled tree model at the same scales (0.874 and 0.869; 0.870 and 0.867), with widely overlapping intervals, so we claim nothing in either direction.

**Why trees, and their price.** Summing clients' gradient and hessian histograms at the server equals a pooled histogram tree model by construction (tested), and the pooled control confirms it: federated and pooled AUROC agree to within 0.001 in five of six settings [Table 13]. The price is communication: about 11 MB per client per round on the strict cohort, 40 to 50 times the total uplink of a federated MLP, and 97 minutes of wall-clock for the search plus three seeds (7.4 hours on all stays) on one machine [Table 9]. The hyperparameter search itself gave no test benefit: the default configuration scored 0.8748 and the searched winner 0.8742. A graph head over patient embeddings gave nothing either (differences at most 0.0001 AUROC), because the embedding already carries the label signal.

**Sepsis.** The sepsis label (an antibiotic plus a SOFA-like score of at least 2) is a proxy, so we restrict the task to the 151 hospitals with at least 80% medication-record coverage, where the antibiotic half can fire, and show the feature ladder from 0.993 (label ingredients allowed) to 0.801 (progressively removed) [Section 3]. The tree model's +0.018 over the federated MLP on the proxy label shows no measurable gain on clinician-documented sepsis, at either window (0.7683 and 0.7796 against 0.7669 and 0.7769 for the federated MLP) [Table 8]. We therefore claim internal validity, early-warning performance and the documented-sepsis check, and not diagnostic accuracy.

**Limitations.** *Design and data.* All results come from one database and one fixed hospital-wise split. The seed standard deviation reflects training randomness only, and the bootstrap intervals reflect test-patient sampling only. In federations of 3 or 10 hospitals, hospital-level inputs identify the site; every model sees them, so comparisons between models are on equal terms, but absolute levels are not comparable with a model trained without them. *Outcome definitions and references.* The sepsis label is a proxy (29% precision and 50% recall against documented sepsis), so we claim internal validity and early-warning performance, not diagnostic accuracy. The comparison with FLICS rests on reported figures from another cohort, label and test set, with a wide reference interval (about ±0.035), and the base paper was chosen after the comparison was known. The label effect [Table 12] comes from a single seed, and the models were trained on in-hospital, not ICU-discharge, death. All-stays AUROC contains a short-stay effect, which is why the strict landmark cohort is the headline. Accuracy and F1 use a validation-chosen threshold and depend on prevalence, so they are not comparable with FLICS's. In the pooled-GBDT control the 3-hospital difference is about the size of the seed spread and is not resolved. *Selection, privacy and cost.* Test results were visible during the FedProx sweep, although selection used validation data and Table 6 shows a plateau. The 6 h tree model was re-run with a higher round limit after its first run reached its cap with test results visible; the rerun was chosen on validation AUROC and both runs are reported [Section 5]. A federated model is not a private model: we used neither differential privacy nor secure aggregation, and the shared histograms may reveal information about local data that we did not measure. The tree model's uplink is about 40 to 50 times that of the federated MLP on the strict cohort.

**Conclusion.** Under a strict, leakage-controlled protocol, federated gradient boosting is the strongest federated model we tested for in-hospital mortality and for the sepsis proxy, within 0.002 to 0.005 AUROC of pooled LightGBM (a gap also present when the same tree code is trained pooled, so not the price of federation) and far above isolated training, at a communication cost that is high but measurable. What remains open is privacy (differential privacy or secure aggregation for the histograms), external validation on another database, and models trained on the ICU-discharge label itself, to learn how much of the gap to earlier studies the outcome definition explains.
